## Strengths

- The dependency table at 218-221 is the clearest statement of the certainty-equivalence result anywhere in the lecture - two rows, `Depends on` and `Independent of`, for $h_1$ and $h_2$ - and it is put to work immediately: 223 reads the policy-analysis consequence straight off it ("since $h_1$ is invariant to $f$, the policy analyst need only re-solve the forecasting problem").
- The lecture is organised as an answer to a quoted question. 76-78 states Hurwicz's problem verbatim, 84-88 restates it as three conditions, 130-138 shows why an estimated decision rule cannot answer it ("this single estimate, however precise, *reveals nothing* about how $T(f)$ varies with $f$"), 140 says what would, and 156-158 names the difficulty that remains - so every section has an identifiable job.
- Citation style is handled correctly and consistently throughout: eleven in-text `{cite:t}` calls where the author names carry the sentence (51, 66, 68, 76, 90, 140, 170, 174, 248, 313, 330) against a single parenthetical `{cite}` at 76, `(`` {cite}`Hurwicz:1962` ``)`.
- The critique of adaptive expectations is made precisely rather than dismissively: 322 concedes the point an unsympathetic reader would raise ("The deficiency is not that `` {eq}`eq:adaptive_expectations_v3` `` is a distributed lag -- linear forecasting rules are perfectly acceptable simplifications"), 324 then locates the actual defect, and 326 names the mapping $h_2 = S(f)$ that supplies the missing restriction.
- The numerical check states its prediction before it runs it: 259-262 says what the CE theorem implies for this experiment - the gain $F$ flat in $\sigma$, the value constant $d$ rising with it - and 306-308 reads the two panels back against those two claims, so the figure is a test rather than an illustration.
- The exercise at 337-353 asks for the sharper version of what the figure showed - not that $d$ rises but that $d \propto \sigma^2$ - and the solution then verifies the constant of proportionality against a $P$ computed from a noiseless problem (375-379), with 384 explaining why substituting $C = 0$ is legitimate ("$P$ is determined entirely by preferences and technology, not by the noise level").

## Recommended actions

1. Either give the numerical experiment an exogenous state so the separation it claims to verify can appear, or retitle 251 to say what it actually shows. As written, 255 has no $z_t$, so $h_2$, $\tilde z_t$ and $S(f)$ - the objects of every section from 176 to 249 - cannot be exercised; an AR(1) $z_t$ entering the transition, solved once under $f_0$ and once under $f_1$ with $h_1$ held fixed, would demonstrate the separation table at 218-221 rather than restate the Overview's variance claim from 62.
2. Resolve $V$: it is the one-period return function at 140, 145 and 149 and the value function at 262. Rename one of them (the code's `P` and `d` already carry the value function, so 262 could say "the additive term in the optimal value function $-y^\top P y - d$"), and change one of the two $r$'s at 255, which uses the same letter for the return function and its own control-cost coefficient in a single display.
3. The drafted `qe-code-002` finding at 376 is a false positive: `beta=β` is the keyword argument of `quantecon.LQ`, not an author-chosen identifier, and the identical kwarg at 285 is correctly not flagged - it is only caught at 376 because the multi-line call puts `beta=β` on a line without the `LQ(` that would exempt it. Do not rename it.
4. Align the $Q$/$R$ matrix naming with the companion lecture. Here 277-278 name the state cost `R_mat` and the control cost `Q_mat`, which follows `quantecon.LQ`; `theil_2`:117 and :150-151 name them the other way round, and each file then compensates in the argument order so the numbers agree. Fix one of the two, or add a note of the kind `phillips_escaping_nash`:93-100 uses to flag its own reversed coefficient ordering.
5. Plot the distributed-lag weights of `` {eq}`eq:adaptive_expectations_v3` `` against the optimal weights implied by an AR(1) environment, for two different environments, so the claim at 326 ("when $f$ changes, $h_2$ changes") is shown rather than asserted.
6. Report how flat the gain is instead of relying on the y-limit: 296 sets `set_ylim(0, 2 * max(F_vals) + 0.1)` and 306 then calls $F$ "*flat* across all noise levels"; one printed `max(F_vals) - min(F_vals)` makes the point unambiguously.
